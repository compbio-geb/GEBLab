Research

We formulate hypotheses about how genomes evolve, test them with functional genomic data, and build the computational tools that testing requires.

Biological data are noisy and biased. Much of our work is about separating real signal from those artifacts, so that conclusions about evolution, gene regulation and disease hold up.

Predicting essential genes

Some genes are required for a cell to survive; most are not. We build variational graph attention models that predict gene essentiality from gene networks and protein structure, and identify the functional substructures that drive each prediction. This work is funded by NIH NIGMS (R15GM165012, 2026–2029).

Gene regulation and noncoding variants

Most disease-associated variants fall outside genes. We study how transcription factors, cis-regulatory elements and three-dimensional genome structure control gene expression, and use those maps to refine eQTL detection and predict the effects of noncoding variants. This builds on our contributions to ENCODE, EN-TEx, GENCODE and modERN, and on an NIH R03 on 3D genome structure and eQTLs.

Molecular evolution at genome scale

We ask classic evolutionary questions with genome-wide data: are human translated pseudogenes functional? How continuous is protein structure space? Why do residues that cause disease in humans appear as the normal amino acid in other species? Our structural evidence supports the compensation hypothesis, in which other changes in the protein neutralize the harmful effect.

Genomics for precision medicine

We apply statistical and deep-learning models to human health questions, including sex-specific genetic networks in cancer, chemotherapy resistance, clinical predictors in sickle cell disease, and long COVID, often in collaboration with clinical researchers at Howard.

Methods we use

Functional and evolutionary genomics, Bayesian statistics, statistical modeling, graph neural networks and other deep-learning approaches, and cloud computing on NIH resources such as All of Us.
